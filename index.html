RESEARCH BRIEF / THREE THREADS

25 MILESTONES · 1950–2025

01 / Historical context

AI developed through symbolic reasoning, trainable networks, expert systems, and statistical learning. Turing, McCarthy, Rosenblatt, Hinton, and later research teams contributed different building blocks. Progress was cumulative, not a single invention in 2022.

02 / Two AI winters

Approximately 1974–1980 and 1987–1993: ambitious promises collided with technical limits and, later, costly and brittle commercial systems. Funding and confidence weakened in parts of the field, but research continued. Dates vary by account and region.

03 / Why recent systems succeed

Deep-learning methods learn useful representations; large datasets support training and comparison; GPUs accelerate computation. Cloud platforms add rentable hardware, storage, networking, and deployment scale. These factors reinforce one another; cloud alone does not create intelligence.

READING THE AXIS: 2022 marks ChatGPT’s public release, not GPT-3’s introduction (2020). Year groups use equal spacing for legibility, not proportional calendar distances. Winter ranges are approximate historical periods, not a claim that all AI research stopped. Hover a titled illustration for causes, impact, people, organizations, and a source link.

1950Turing: Can Machines Think?
Historical Context		Alan Turing introduces the imitation game in Computing Machinery and Intelligence. It reframes machine intelligence around observable conversational behavior, establishing a lasting question for AI research.
Key figure: Alan Turing. Institution: University of Manchester.		Reference ↗
1956Dartmouth: AI Takes Shape
Historical Context		The Dartmouth summer research project helps establish artificial intelligence as a research field. Organizers John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude Shannon propose studying how machines might simulate aspects of intelligence.
Institution: Dartmouth College. The proposal dates to 1955; the workshop takes place in 1956.		Reference ↗
1958Rosenblatt’s Perceptron
Historical Context		Frank Rosenblatt demonstrates the perceptron, an early trainable neural system for pattern recognition. It shows that machine behavior can be learned from examples rather than specified entirely by hand.
Key figure: Frank Rosenblatt. Institution: Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory. Early single-layer models have important limitations.		Reference ↗
1974–1980 (approx.)First AI Winter · c. 1974–1980
AI Winter		CAUSES: Early promises exceeded practical results; limited computing and combinatorial complexity restricted useful applications. The 1973 Lighthill report intensified criticism in the UK.
IMPACTS: Confidence and support weakened in parts of the field, with a separate US trajectory. Research continued. The period and the report’s direct funding effects are debated.		Reference ↗
1980Expert Systems Revive Interest
Historical Context		During the 1980s, rule-based expert systems attract commercial interest by encoding specialist knowledge for bounded tasks. Their practical uses revive enthusiasm after the first winter.
Organizations: AI research groups and enterprise adopters. Limitation: hand-built rules are difficult to maintain and do not automatically generalize.		Reference ↗
1986Backpropagation Gains Momentum
Deep Learning		David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton, and Ronald Williams demonstrate how error backpropagation trains internal representations in multilayer neural networks. Their influential paper helps popularize a method central to later deep learning.
This is a major dissemination milestone, not the first invention of every form of backpropagation.		Reference ↗
1987–1993 (approx.)Second AI Winter · c. 1987–1993
AI Winter		CAUSES: Brittle, expensive expert systems struggled to meet commercial expectations; the specialized Lisp-machine market weakened as cheaper computers improved.
IMPACTS: Investment and enthusiasm contracted, and some AI ventures failed. Work in learning and other subfields continued. The end date is approximate; some accounts extend the winter further into the 1990s.		Reference ↗
1997Deep Blue Defeats Kasparov
Historical Context		IBM’s Deep Blue beats world chess champion Garry Kasparov in a six-game match. Specialized search, chess expertise, and dedicated hardware deliver a landmark result in a narrowly defined task.
Key people: Feng-hsiung Hsu, Murray Campbell, and the IBM team. This is not a general-intelligence system.		Reference ↗
2006Deep Networks Regain Momentum
Deep Learning		Geoffrey Hinton, Simon Osindero, and Yee-Whye Teh publish a layer-by-layer learning method for deep belief networks. The work helps renew interest in training networks with multiple hidden layers.
Institution: University of Toronto. It is one strand of the deep-learning revival, not the sole origin of modern AI.		Reference ↗
2009ImageNet Supplies Training Data
Deep Learning		Jia Deng, Fei-Fei Li, and collaborators present ImageNet, a large labeled image database. Shared datasets and benchmarks make it easier to train and compare visual recognition systems.
Institution: Princeton University at the time of the paper. Contribution: scalable data infrastructure, alongside advances in algorithms and hardware.		Reference ↗
2012AlexNet: The GPU Breakthrough
Deep Learning		Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton achieve a major ImageNet competition victory with a deep convolutional network trained on NVIDIA GPUs.
Institution: University of Toronto. Why it matters: labeled data, deep networks, and GPU computation combine to sharply improve image recognition. Cloud computing was not the direct training setup for this result.		Reference ↗
2015TensorFlow Opens the Toolkit
Deep Learning & Cloud		Google releases TensorFlow as open-source software. Its machine-learning system supports computation across heterogeneous devices and distributed environments, helping researchers and developers build and deploy models.
Key figures: Jeff Dean, Rajat Monga, and the Google team. The release broadens access to practical deep-learning tools.		Reference ↗
2016AlphaGo Masters a Complex Game
Deep Learning		Google DeepMind’s AlphaGo defeats Lee Sedol 4–1. The system combines deep neural networks, reinforcement learning, and tree search.
Key figures: David Silver, Demis Hassabis, and the DeepMind team. Why it matters: learned evaluation and search succeed in a decision space too large for straightforward exhaustive enumeration.		Reference ↗
2016AWS Brings GPUs to the Cloud
Cloud Computing		Amazon Web Services launches EC2 P2 instances with NVIDIA Tesla K80 GPUs, explicitly supporting deep learning and other compute-intensive workloads.
Companies: AWS and NVIDIA. Why it matters: organizations can rent GPU capacity rather than purchase all hardware upfront. This improves access and flexibility; it does not eliminate training costs or infrastructure constraints.		Reference ↗
2017Transformers Reshape Language AI
Deep Learning		Ashish Vaswani and collaborators introduce the Transformer in Attention Is All You Need. Attention replaces recurrence in their sequence-transduction architecture, allowing more parallelizable training.
Organizations: Google Brain and Google Research. Why it matters: the architecture becomes a foundation for many later large language models.		Reference ↗
2020Azure Builds AI Supercomputing
Cloud Computing		Microsoft announces an Azure-hosted supercomputer built for OpenAI, with over 285,000 CPU cores and 10,000 GPUs.
Companies: Microsoft and OpenAI. Why it matters: high-speed networking and large compute clusters support training very large models. Cloud infrastructure complements algorithms and data; it is not an algorithmic breakthrough by itself.		Reference ↗
2020GPT-3 Learns from Prompts
Recent Successes		OpenAI presents GPT-3, a 175-billion-parameter language model that performs a range of tasks through prompts and few-shot examples.
Key figures: Tom Brown and the paper’s coauthors. Why it matters: scaling and in-context learning reduce the need for task-specific fine-tuning in many demonstrations, while factual and reasoning limitations remain.		Reference ↗
2020AlphaFold 2 Advances Science
Recent Successes		DeepMind’s AlphaFold 2 achieves a major protein-structure prediction breakthrough at CASP14.
Key figures: Demis Hassabis, John Jumper, and the DeepMind team. Why it matters: deep learning becomes a powerful scientific tool, drawing on protein data, architectural advances, and substantial computation. Predictions still require scientific interpretation.		Reference ↗
2022ChatGPT: The Public Turning Point
Recent Successes		On November 30, OpenAI launches ChatGPT as a research preview based on a GPT-3.5-series model. A conversational interface makes generative AI easier for the public to use.
Company: OpenAI. This is the center of the timeline because of its public impact. GPT-3 was introduced in 2020, not 2022. Outputs can still be inaccurate.		Reference ↗
2023GPT-4 Extends Model Capabilities
Recent Successes		OpenAI introduces GPT-4 on March 14, improving performance on many complex tasks and describing image-input capabilities alongside text. Access to features rolls out in stages.
Company: OpenAI. Why it matters: it expands the range of tasks for large models without establishing reliable general intelligence or eliminating hallucinations.		Reference ↗
2023Llama 2 Broadens Model Access
Deep Learning & Cloud		Meta releases Llama 2 model weights under its license, with Microsoft as a preferred partner and availability through Azure. Developers gain more options to adapt and deploy language models.
Companies: Meta and Microsoft. The custom license has conditions; available weights should not be confused with unrestricted open-source licensing.		Reference ↗
2024GPT-4o Connects Voice and Vision
Recent Successes		OpenAI introduces GPT-4o, designed to work across text, images, and audio. Demonstrations emphasize low-latency interaction, with product capabilities released in stages.
Company: OpenAI. Why it matters: multimodal deep learning supports more natural interfaces while large-scale serving infrastructure brings those capabilities to users.		Reference ↗
2024o1 Adds Reasoning-Time Compute
Recent Successes		OpenAI releases o1-preview, trained with reinforcement learning to spend additional computation reasoning before answering.
Company: OpenAI. Why it matters: improvements on difficult mathematics, science, and coding tasks show that progress can come from how computation is used at inference time, not only from larger training runs.		Reference ↗
2024AI’s Scientific Impact Recognized
Recent Successes		The Nobel Prize in Physics honors John Hopfield and Geoffrey Hinton for foundational neural-network discoveries. In Chemistry, Demis Hassabis and John Jumper receive half the prize for protein-structure prediction; David Baker receives the other half for computational protein design.
Organizations include Princeton, the University of Toronto, Google DeepMind, and the University of Washington.		Reference ↗
2025AI Enters Research Workflows
Recent Successes		OpenAI launches deep research, which searches online sources and synthesizes cited reports across multiple steps.
Company: OpenAI. Why it matters: language models, tool use, and hosted computation combine into longer workflows. Source checking and human judgment remain essential; citations do not guarantee that every claim is correct.		Reference ↗
